# Module 8 — Building a Trading Plan

*"Turning Everything You've Learned Into Written Rules"*

## Module Introduction

Every module so far has taught you a piece of a trader's decision-making:
reading charts (Module 2), using indicators (Module 3), reading structure
(Module 4), reading fundamental context (Module 5), sizing and managing
risk (Module 6), and following a process under emotional pressure (Module
7). This module is where all of that gets assembled into one thing: a
**written trading plan** — a single document specific enough that you
could hand it to someone else and they'd understand exactly how you
trade.

This module is built as a workshop, not a lecture. By the end of it,
you'll have produced an actual first draft of your own trading plan,
using the templates and worksheets built into each lesson. There is no
single "correct" trading plan this module is steering you toward —
different trading styles, schedules, and personalities produce
genuinely different, equally legitimate plans. What every good plan
shares isn't its specific content; it's that the content is written down,
specific, and followed consistently.

One thing this module will not do: claim that having a written plan
guarantees profitable trading. A plan doesn't remove the uncertainty
every trade carries (Module 6), and it doesn't remove the emotional
pressure to deviate from it (Module 7) — what it does is give you
something specific to follow, and something specific to test and improve
against, which Module 9 (Backtesting & Trading Journal) teaches you to do
properly. Writing a plan is not the same as validating one; this module
produces a first draft meant to be tested, not a finished, proven system.

The course's overall philosophy applies directly to where this module
sits:

```
LEARN → UNDERSTAND → PRACTICE → BACKTEST → JOURNAL → IMPROVE
```

Modules 1–7 covered LEARN and UNDERSTAND. This module is where PRACTICE
starts to take a concrete, written shape. Module 9 covers BACKTEST and
JOURNAL, and Module 10 covers IMPROVE as an ongoing, ongoing process.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Explain what a trading plan is, and how it differs from a strategy and
  from a single trading idea.
- Choose a trading style (scalping, day trading, swing trading, or
  position trading) suited to your own time, schedule, and experience.
- Define the specific markets, timeframes, and setup conditions your plan
  will use, in objective, checkable language.
- Write entry rules, stop-loss and take-profit rules, and risk rules that
  are specific enough to follow consistently.
- Write rules for how your plan handles scheduled news and high-impact
  events.
- Build a complete before/during/after trading schedule.
- Write explicit no-trade conditions, and explain why declining to trade
  is itself a valid, planned decision.
- Assemble every component above into one complete, written trading-plan
  document, using this module's template.
- Audit a trading plan for vague or conflicting rules, and explain why a
  plan needs to be tested rather than assumed to be good on sight.

### Lesson 1 — What Is a Trading Plan?

A **trading plan** is a written, specific set of rules covering every
major decision a trader makes — what to trade, when to trade, what to
look for, when to enter, where to exit, how much to risk, when not to
trade, and how to review performance. The short version, worth
remembering for the rest of this module:

**A trading plan turns intentions into rules.**

"I want to trade based on trend and structure" is an intention. "I enter
only when price closes beyond a marked swing high in the direction of
higher-timeframe structure, per Module 4, Lesson 7's BOS definition" is a
rule — specific enough to check, after the fact, whether it was actually
followed.

**Why written rules matter:** a rule that only exists in a trader's head
is negotiable in the moment, in a way a written rule is not. Module 7
covered exactly how fear, greed, FOMO, and revenge trading distort
in-the-moment decisions — a written plan is the concrete reference point
those lessons' frameworks (the Pre-Trade Psychology Check, for one) are
checked against. Without something written down, "did I follow my
rules?" has no clear answer, because the rules themselves were never
pinned down.

**A strategy vs. a complete trading plan:** a **strategy** is the
market-reading logic — the specific technical, price-action, or
fundamental conditions that define a setup (Modules 2–5). A **trading
plan** is broader: it includes the strategy, but also the risk rules
(Module 6), the behavioral rules (Module 7), the schedule, the news
rules, and the no-trade conditions. A trader can have a sound strategy
and still lack a complete plan, if none of that surrounding structure is
written down.

**A trading plan vs. a trading idea:** a **trading idea** is a single,
specific instance — "I think EUR/USD is going to rally because of X" — a
one-off view about one trade. A trading plan is the repeatable process
that produces and evaluates many trading ideas over time, consistently,
using the same criteria each time.

**Why plans reduce impulsive decision-making:** a written plan moves
several decisions out of the emotionally-charged moment of an open
position and into a calmer moment, in advance, when a trader isn't
currently affected by a live trade's outcome. Module 7 called this the
gap between what you decide in advance and what you feel like doing in
the moment — a written plan is the concrete artifact that closes that
gap, because it exists and can be checked, rather than being reconstructed
from memory under pressure.

**What a complete plan should contain, at a glance** — this module builds
each of these in turn, one lesson at a time:

```
WHAT I TRADE → WHEN I TRADE → WHAT I LOOK FOR → WHEN I ENTER →
WHERE I EXIT → HOW MUCH I RISK → WHEN I DO NOT TRADE → HOW I REVIEW
```

> **Beginner mistake:** treating a strategy alone ("I trade breakouts")
> as if it were already a complete plan, without the risk rules,
> schedule, news rules, and no-trade conditions that turn a market-reading
> idea into something actually repeatable day to day.

### Lesson 2 — Choosing Your Trading Style

A **trading style** describes how long a trader typically holds a
position and how frequently they trade — the broad category your specific
setups (Lesson 5) will operate inside.

**The four broad styles, compared:**

| Style | Typical holding period | Timeframes commonly used | Time commitment | Potential setups |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Scalping | Seconds to minutes | 1-minute to 5-minute | Very high — near-constant screen time during sessions | Many per day |
| Day trading | Minutes to hours, closed same day | 5-minute to 1-hour | High during active sessions | Several per day to a few per week |
| Swing trading | Days to a few weeks | 4-hour to daily | Moderate — periodic checking, not constant | A handful per week to per month |
| Position trading | Weeks to months | Daily to weekly | Low — infrequent checking | A few per month to per quarter |

**These are broad categories, not rigid boxes.** Real trading often
blends elements of two adjacent styles, and the exact boundary between
"day trading" and "swing trading," for instance, isn't fixed anywhere —
what matters is that your own plan states, specifically, which one (or
which blend) you're actually running, so your timeframe and schedule
choices (Lessons 4 and 10) stay consistent with it.

**Choosing a style based on your own situation, not a "best" style:**

- **Available time and schedule** — scalping and day trading demand real-
  time attention during specific sessions; swing and position trading fit
  around a schedule that can't accommodate constant monitoring.
- **Personality and preferences** — some traders find frequent,
  fast decisions energizing; others find the same pace exhausting and
  prefer the slower pace of fewer, more spaced-out decisions.
- **Ability to monitor markets** — a style that requires watching price
  during hours you can't actually watch it is not a viable style for you,
  regardless of its theoretical merits.
- **Experience level** — a beginner still building the pattern-recognition
  skills from Modules 2–4 often benefits from the slower decision pace of
  swing trading, simply because there's more time to think through each
  decision before it's needed — though this course does not prescribe
  swing trading as mandatory.

**This course does not claim any one style is superior.** Each style
suits different life circumstances and different traders; a profitable
swing trader and a profitable day trader are both possible outcomes of
sound process, applied to a style that actually fits the trader running
it.

> **Beginner mistake:** choosing a style based on what looks exciting or
> what's commonly discussed online, rather than an honest assessment of
> how much real-time attention you can actually give the markets during
> the sessions that style requires.

### Lesson 3 — Choosing Markets and Currency Pairs

This lesson defines exactly which instruments your plan will focus on —
a deliberately narrow, written decision, not an open-ended "whatever
looks interesting today."

**Majors, minors, and exotics, briefly revisited from Module 1:** major
pairs (like EUR/USD, GBP/USD, USD/JPY) generally offer the tightest
spreads and highest liquidity; minor pairs (crosses not involving USD)
and exotic pairs (involving a smaller or less-traded economy's currency)
often carry wider spreads and can behave less predictably around news
(Module 5).

**Why beginners may benefit from limiting their watchlist:** tracking
many pairs at once means dividing attention across many charts, many
sets of news events (Module 5), and many correlated or uncorrelated
setups — often at the direct cost of applying Modules 2–4's analysis
carefully to any single one. A shorter, deliberately chosen watchlist
lets a trader actually get to know a pair's typical behavior, session
patterns, and typical spread — knowledge that's diluted across a longer
list.

**Liquidity and spread considerations:** Module 1, Lesson 5 already
covered the spread as a real, immediate trading cost. Pairs with wider,
less consistent spreads make Module 6's risk calculations less precise
and add cost to every entry and exit — a real factor in deciding which
instruments belong on a beginner's watchlist.

**Correlated positions:** some pairs move in a closely related way to
each other (sharing a common currency, or reacting to overlapping
fundamental drivers, per Module 5). Holding several correlated positions
at once can mean taking on more combined risk than it first appears —
Module 6, Lesson 12's "maximum simultaneous exposure" field is exactly
where a plan accounts for this.

**Why watching too many instruments creates unnecessary complexity:**
each additional pair adds its own chart to read, its own news calendar to
check (Module 5, Lesson 9), and its own typical spread and session
behavior to learn — complexity that doesn't necessarily produce more
good setups, just more to track.

**Market Selection Worksheet — fill this in as part of your plan:**

| Instrument | Why Trade It | Session | Main Timeframe | Typical Conditions | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *(example)* EUR/USD | Tight spreads, high liquidity, familiar behavior | London/New York overlap | 1-hour | Typically 0.5–1.5 pip spread | Primary pair while building experience |

**This course does not prescribe a universally correct number of
pairs.** One pair, traded well, is a completely legitimate choice for a
beginner; so is a deliberately chosen handful. What matters is that the
list is a deliberate, written decision — not simply whatever happens to
be on screen.

> **Beginner mistake:** trading whatever pair happens to be moving that
> day, rather than sticking to a deliberately chosen, written watchlist
> the trader has actually studied.

### Lesson 4 — Choosing Timeframes

**Timeframe selection** means deciding, in writing, which chart
timeframes your plan actually uses, and for what purpose — building
directly on Module 2, Lesson 13's multi-timeframe introduction and Module
4, Lesson 11's higher-timeframe-context framework.

**Higher vs. lower timeframes, and top-down analysis:** a higher
timeframe (daily, 4-hour) shows the broader context — overall trend and
structure (Module 4). A lower timeframe (1-hour, 15-minute) shows the
detail needed for a precise entry. **Top-down analysis** means checking
the higher timeframe first to establish context, then moving to lower
timeframes to refine the actual entry — the same direction Module 4,
Lesson 11 taught for reading structure across timeframes.

**A simple framework: Context → Setup → Entry**

- **Context** — the highest timeframe your plan checks, establishing the
  broader trend or structure (per Module 4).
- **Setup** — a middle timeframe where your specific setup conditions
  (Lesson 5) are actually evaluated.
- **Entry** — the timeframe your specific entry trigger (Lesson 6) is
  read from, often the shortest one your plan uses.

**Worked example:** a swing trader (Lesson 2) might define: Context =
daily chart (is structure bullish, bearish, or ranging?); Setup = 4-hour
chart (is a defined setup, like a pullback to a marked zone, actually
present?); Entry = 1-hour chart (has a specific trigger, like a bullish
engulfing candle at that zone, actually formed?). A scalper's version of
the same framework would simply use shorter timeframes throughout — for
example, 15-minute context, 5-minute setup, 1-minute entry — while
keeping the same three-layer logic.

**Timeframe consistency:** once a plan states which timeframes it uses
for context, setup, and entry, that assignment should stay fixed across
trades — not chosen fresh each time based on which timeframe happens to
currently support the trade a trader wants to take.

**Avoiding random timeframe switching:** flipping between timeframes
mid-analysis, specifically looking for one that shows a picture
supporting a decision already made, undermines the entire point of a
written, repeatable plan — it's a variation of the "timeframe shopping"
mistake Module 4, Lesson 11 named directly.

> **Beginner mistake:** using a different set of timeframes on every
> trade depending on what "looks right" in the moment, rather than fixing
> a specific context/setup/entry combination in the written plan and
> using it consistently.

### Lesson 5 — Defining Your Trading Setup

A **setup**, defined for a trading plan, is a specific, checkable set of
market conditions that must be present before a trade is even considered
— the written version of everything Modules 2–5 taught you to look for.

**What a setup definition should include:**

- **Market conditions** — is the instrument trending, ranging, or
  transitioning (Module 2, Lessons 8–9)?
- **Trend/range context** — the higher-timeframe read from Lesson 4's
  "Context" step.
- **Technical or price-action conditions** — specific indicator readings
  (Module 3) or structural conditions (Module 4) that must be present.
- **Fundamental/news considerations** — anything from Module 5 the setup
  depends on or needs to account for (Lesson 9 covers this fully).
- **Required confluences** — the specific combination of the above that
  must line up (Module 3, Lesson 10's confluence concept).

**Required conditions vs. optional conditions:** a **required condition**
must be present for the setup to be valid at all — its absence
automatically rules the trade out. An **optional condition** (sometimes
called supporting confluence) strengthens a setup when present but isn't
independently necessary. Separating these two categories in writing
avoids a common trap: treating every piece of supporting evidence as
equally mandatory, which can make a setup so narrow it almost never
occurs, or so loosely defined that almost anything qualifies.

**Why vague rules are difficult to test consistently:** a rule like "the
chart looks good" or "momentum feels strong" cannot be checked
objectively after the fact — two different days, or two different
traders, could read the exact same chart as meeting or not meeting that
standard. A setup defined in observable terms — a specific structural
condition (Module 4), a specific indicator reading (Module 3), a
specific zone (Module 4, Lesson 5) — can be checked the same way every
time, which is what makes it possible to actually track and improve over
time (Module 9).

**Scenario — a setup that meets most but not all required conditions:**
a trader's written setup requires three things: bullish higher-timeframe
structure, a pullback to a marked demand zone, and a confirming bullish
candle at that zone (per Module 4). On a specific trade, the first two
conditions are clearly present, but the confirming candle hasn't formed
— price is still sitting inside the zone without a clear signal yet.

**Working through the decision:** per this lesson's required-vs-optional
distinction, if the confirming candle is a *required* condition in this
trader's written setup, the trade is not yet valid — regardless of how
promising the first two conditions look. The rule exists specifically for
moments like this one, where two out of three conditions can create
pressure to treat the setup as "close enough." A trader following their
plan waits for the third condition or lets the trade go; a trader
overriding their plan enters anyway, on the reasoning that the first two
conditions felt sufficient.

**Setup Definition Worksheet — fill this in for each setup your plan
uses:**

| Field | Your answer |
|---|---|
| Setup name | |
| Market conditions | |
| Context (higher timeframe read) | |
| Required conditions | |
| Optional confluences | |
| Entry trigger | |
| Invalidation condition | |
| Exit condition | |
| No-trade condition | |

> **Beginner mistake:** writing a setup definition using subjective
> language ("looks strong," "feels like a good level") instead of the
> specific, observable conditions Modules 2–5 actually taught — a rule
> that can't be checked can't really be followed or tested either.

### Lesson 6 — Entry Rules

**Entry rules** define exactly when a valid setup (Lesson 5) actually
becomes a trade — building directly on Module 4, Lesson 9's entry
framework.

**What entry rules should define:**

- **Exact entry conditions** — the specific price action or condition
  that triggers entry, stated precisely enough to be checked afterward.
- **Confirmation requirements** — per Module 4, Lesson 9, whether this
  plan uses confirmation entries (waiting for a condition to actually
  occur) or anticipation entries (acting before it does), and under what
  circumstances.
- **Entry triggers** — the specific, observable event (a candle close, a
  break of a level) that actually triggers the entry, decided in advance.
- **Market vs. pending orders** — per Module 4, Lesson 9, whether this
  setup calls for entering immediately once triggered, or placing a
  pending order at a specific level.
- **What must happen before entering** — the full checklist below.
- **What invalidates an entry** — the specific condition that means the
  setup no longer applies, so the trader stops looking for the entry
  rather than continuing to wait for a condition that's no longer valid.
- **Avoiding emotional entries** — per Module 7, an entry driven by FOMO,
  revenge, or impatience is not the same thing as an entry that met this
  lesson's actual criteria, however similar they might look from the
  outside.

**Scenario — vague entry language:** a trader's written plan states:
"enter when momentum looks strong." Reviewing this rule honestly: what,
specifically, does "looks strong" mean? Two different days, this same
trader might read identical momentum readings (Module 3, Lesson 4)
completely differently, depending on mood or recent results (Module 7's
recency bias) — the rule provides no actual, checkable standard.
Rewritten in observable terms, matching Lesson 5's required-conditions
approach, this might instead read: "enter when RSI crosses above 50 on
the entry timeframe, following a confirmed bullish BOS on the setup
timeframe (Module 4, Lesson 7)" — specific enough that any two readings
of the same chart would reach the same conclusion about whether the rule
was met.

**Entry Checklist — an example structure, not a universal rule:**

1. Market context confirmed (Lesson 4's "Context" step)
2. Setup conditions present (Lesson 5's required conditions)
3. Entry trigger present (this lesson's specific trigger)
4. News/calendar checked (Lesson 9)
5. Risk acceptable (Lesson 8, building on Module 6)
6. Stop-loss location defined (Lesson 7)
7. Trade meets the written plan, with no exceptions made
8. Execute, or pass

Working through a checklist like this before every entry — not just
recalling it from memory — is what actually turns Module 7's Pre-Trade
Psychology Check into a habit rather than a one-time idea.

> **Beginner mistake:** writing entry rules using words that describe a
> feeling rather than an observable market condition — "looks strong,"
> "seems ready," "feels right" are not entry triggers, they're
> descriptions of the trader's own state of mind.

### Lesson 7 — Stop Loss, Take Profit, and Exit Rules

This lesson defines how a plan handles exits — where a stop loss
conceptually belongs and how a take profit or other exit gets decided —
building on Module 4 (Lessons 9–10) and Module 6, without repeating
Module 6's dollar-risk or position-sizing calculations.

**Where a stop loss belongs, conceptually:** per Module 4, Lesson 10, a
stop loss belongs at the point that proves the original trade idea
wrong — not at an arbitrary distance. A plan's stop-loss rule should
state, in writing, *how* that point gets identified for this specific
setup (a structural level, a zone boundary), not a fixed number of pips
used regardless of the setup.

**Structural invalidation:** the specific price level, tied to the
setup's own logic (Module 4), that would mean the reasoning behind the
trade no longer holds — the same concept Module 4, Lesson 10 introduced,
written into the plan as a rule rather than decided fresh each time.

**Planned exit conditions and take-profit methods:** a plan can define
its take profit several different ways, and should state which one it
uses:

- A fixed structural target (a prior swing point, per Module 4).
- A fixed risk-to-reward ratio (Module 6, Lesson 6) applied to the
  stop-loss distance.
- A combination — a structural target used as long as it implies at
  least a minimum acceptable ratio.

**Partial exits:** some plans close part of a position at one target and
let the remainder run toward a further one — a choice that should be
written into the plan specifically (what percentage, at what level), not
decided mid-trade.

**Trailing stops:** a stop that moves in the trade's favor as price
moves favorably, locking in progress without exiting entirely — if a
plan uses one, it should state the specific rule for how the trail moves
(for example, "moved to breakeven once price reaches a 1:1 ratio"), not
"moved when it feels safe to."

**Time-based exits:** some setups, especially in faster styles (Lesson
2), include a rule for closing a trade after a set amount of time
regardless of price, if the anticipated move hasn't materialized.

**Conditions for manually closing a trade before the stop or target is
reached:** a plan can allow for this, but should state specifically under
what conditions — for example, "a confirmed CHOCH against the position's
direction" (Module 4, Lesson 8) — rather than leaving it to in-the-moment
judgment alone.

**Why moving exits emotionally undermines a plan:** Module 6, Lesson 3
and Module 7, Lessons 2–3 already covered this from the risk and
psychology angles — moving a stop or target because of how a trade feels,
rather than because a written rule calls for it, is exactly the gap
between plan and behavior this whole module exists to close.

**This course does not prescribe one universal exit method.** A
structural target, a fixed ratio, a trailing stop, or a partial-exit
approach can each be part of a sound plan — what matters is that the
plan states, specifically, which one it uses and why, and that Module
6's actual risk and reward-ratio calculations are used to evaluate it.

> **Beginner mistake:** leaving the exit method undefined until a trade
> is already open, effectively deciding the exit rule in the same
> emotionally-charged moment this module is built to move decisions away
> from.

### Lesson 8 — Risk Rules Inside the Trading Plan

This lesson connects the trading plan directly to Module 6, without
repeating its calculations — this is about *where* those calculations
live inside a written plan, not how to perform them.

**The principle this lesson is built around:**

**The trading plan should define risk BEFORE the trade is entered.**

**What a plan's risk section should include, referencing Module 6 for the
actual math:**

- **Maximum planned risk per trade** — the risk percentage decided in
  advance (Module 6, Lesson 2), stated as a specific written number.
- **Maximum exposure** — the total risk a trader allows across multiple
  simultaneously open positions (Module 6, Lesson 12), relevant
  especially when Lesson 3's correlated pairs are involved.
- **Stop-loss requirement** — a rule stating that every trade must have a
  stop loss defined before entry, with no exceptions (Module 6, Lesson
  3).
- **Position-sizing requirement** — a rule stating that Module 6, Lesson
  5's calculation is run for every trade, every time, rather than
  eyeballing a lot size.
- **Daily/session loss boundaries as a possible planning consideration**
  — a maximum loss that, if reached, ends trading for that session or day
  (Module 6, Lesson 12's template already introduced this field).
- **Conditions under which the trader should stop trading** — connects
  directly to Lesson 11's no-trade rules.

**This course does not prescribe a universal risk percentage** — Module
6, Lesson 2 already established that 0.5%, 1%, and 2% are all commonly
used starting points, not a single correct answer, and this module
doesn't repeat or override that position. What this lesson adds is
simply that whatever number a trader chooses belongs *in the written
plan*, decided before trading begins, not re-decided trade by trade.

**This lesson does not repeat Module 6's detailed position-sizing
calculations.** If you need a refresher on how to calculate a specific
position size from a risk percentage and stop-loss distance, that's
Module 6, Lesson 5 — this lesson only covers where that calculation's
result (a specific risk percentage, applied consistently) belongs inside
the written plan.

> **Beginner mistake:** treating risk management as something handled
> separately from the trading plan, in the trader's head, rather than
> writing the specific risk percentage and rules directly into the same
> document as the entry and exit rules.

### Lesson 9 — Fundamental and News Rules

This lesson defines how a plan accounts for scheduled economic
information — building on Module 5 without re-teaching it.

**What a plan's news section should address:**

- **Economic calendar** — per Module 5, Lesson 9, whether and how often
  the trader checks it before trading.
- **High-impact news, central-bank events, and major economic releases**
  — per Module 5, Lessons 8 and 10, how the plan handles the specific
  volatility these events can create.
- **Trading before and after major announcements** — whether the plan
  allows entries close to a scheduled release, and if so, under what
  conditions.
- **Situations where spreads or volatility may change** — per Module 5,
  Lesson 10, a written acknowledgment that execution conditions around
  major news can differ from ordinary conditions.

**Different approaches a plan can take — this course does not prescribe
one as universally correct:**

- **Avoiding certain news windows entirely** — closing or not opening
  positions for a defined period around specific high-impact releases.
- **Trading only after volatility settles** — waiting a defined period
  after a release before considering new entries.
- **Including news as part of the setup itself** — treating a scheduled
  release, and the market's reaction to it, as a specific condition a
  setup can require or account for.
- **Treating news as context rather than a signal** — using Module 5's
  fundamental backdrop (Module 5, Lesson 12) to inform a trader's overall
  bias, without treating any single release as a standalone entry trigger.

**Scenario — a setup appearing shortly before major news:** a trader's
setup meets every one of their entry criteria (Lesson 5), but a
high-impact release (per Module 5's economic calendar, Lesson 9) is
scheduled in twenty minutes.

**Working through the decision using a written news rule:** without a
written rule, this becomes an in-the-moment judgment call, made under
some time pressure. With a written rule — say, "no new entries within 30
minutes of a high-impact release for the traded currency" — the decision
is already made: the trader passes on this specific entry, not because
the setup was invalid, but because the plan's news rule, decided calmly
in advance, applies here. A different trader's plan might instead
include a rule like "wait 15 minutes after the release, then evaluate
whether the setup still holds" — an equally legitimate, different
approach, as long as it's written down and applied consistently rather
than decided fresh under time pressure.

> **Beginner mistake:** having no written news rule at all, and deciding,
> for the first time, in the moment a scheduled release is approaching,
> whether to take a trade — exactly the kind of in-the-moment decision
> this entire module exists to move earlier, into a calmer moment.

### Lesson 10 — Trading Schedule and Routine

This lesson builds a written trading schedule — connecting to, but not
repeating, Module 7, Lesson 12's psychology-focused routine.

**Trading sessions, briefly revisited from Module 1:** different session
overlaps (London, New York, Tokyo, Sydney) offer different typical
liquidity and volatility for different pairs (Module 1, Lesson 10). A
plan should state which session(s) it actually trades during, tied to
the pairs chosen in Lesson 3.

**Trading Schedule — Before, During, and After:**

**Before Session**

- Calendar check (Module 5, Lesson 9; Lesson 9 above)
- Market review (current structure and context, per Lesson 4)
- Setup review (re-reading the written setup definitions from Lesson 5)
- Risk review (confirming the day's risk boundaries, per Lesson 8)

**During Session**

- Wait for a setup that meets the written criteria (Lesson 5)
- Validate entry against the written entry rules (Lesson 6)
- Execute according to the plan, with no in-the-moment exceptions
- Avoid impulsive changes to an open trade's stop or target (Lesson 7)

**After Session**

- Record the trade(s) taken, or the decision not to trade
- Review adherence — did today's decisions actually follow the written
  plan?
- Note any rule violations specifically (Module 7, Lesson 14's
  Rule-Violation Log is the tool for this)
- Close the session deliberately, rather than continuing to check charts
  after trading is done for the day

**Scenario — a valid setup appearing outside the chosen session:** a
trader's written plan focuses on the London/New York overlap. A setup
that appears to meet their criteria forms several hours later, during a
session their plan doesn't cover.

**Working through the decision:** per this lesson's schedule, the
trader's plan doesn't just define *what* to trade — it defines *when*.
A setup outside the chosen session either falls under a specific written
exception (if the plan has one) or it doesn't qualify, not because the
setup itself is necessarily invalid, but because trading outside a
chosen, familiar session introduces conditions (different typical
liquidity, different typical spread, per Module 1) the trader hasn't
specifically planned for. Passing on it is a legitimate application of
the written schedule, not a missed opportunity in the sense Module 7,
Lesson 4 warned against — it's the schedule doing exactly what it's for.

**When not to trade, and avoiding unnecessary chart watching:** a
schedule also defines when trading *stops* for the day — both to prevent
Module 7, Lesson 6's overtrading and to create the deliberate breaks
Module 7, Lesson 11 recommended after difficult sessions.

> **Beginner mistake:** treating the trading schedule as optional once
> a session is underway — checking charts "just once more" outside the
> planned window, which quietly reopens the door to exactly the
> overtrading and impulsive decisions Module 7 covered.

### Lesson 11 — No-Trade Rules

This is one of the most important lessons in this module: a professional
plan needs explicit, written conditions for when **not** to trade — not
just conditions for when to.

**Why no-trade rules matter as much as entry rules:** every lesson so far
has defined when a trade is valid. This lesson defines the other half —
when a trade, however tempting, is explicitly ruled out. Without written
no-trade conditions, a trader is left making that judgment call fresh,
under whatever pressure is present at the time — precisely the situation
Module 7 covers.

**Common no-trade conditions:**

- **Setup criteria not met** — the most basic case: Lesson 5's required
  conditions simply aren't present.
- **Risk cannot be defined** — Module 6's position-sizing calculation
  depends on knowing the risk amount and stop distance; if either can't
  be pinned down, the trade can't be properly sized.
- **Stop-loss location is unclear** — see the scenario below.
- **Major news creates unacceptable conditions** — per Lesson 9's written
  news rule.
- **The trader has violated their own process recently** — a specific,
  written trigger (for example, "after two rule violations in a session,
  stop trading for the day") connecting to Module 7, Lesson 14's
  Rule-Violation Log.
- **Market conditions don't match the strategy** — for example, a
  trend-following setup during a clearly ranging market (Module 2, Lesson
  9).
- **Spread or execution conditions are unsuitable** — unusually wide
  spreads (Module 1, Lesson 5; Module 5, Lesson 10) that make the
  planned risk-to-reward math (Module 6) unfavorable before the trade
  even starts.
- **The trader is emotionally compromised or acting impulsively** — per
  Module 7, a recognized pattern (fear, greed, FOMO, revenge, impatience)
  currently present and undealt with.

**Scenario — a setup with no clear stop-loss location:** a trader
identifies what otherwise looks like a promising setup, but the price
structure around it (Module 4) doesn't offer a clean, logical point to
place an invalidation-based stop — the nearby swing points are either too
close (making the position size absurdly small) or too far and vague to
represent genuine invalidation.

**Working through the decision:** per Lesson 7, a stop loss belongs at
the point that proves the trade idea wrong — if that point can't be
identified clearly, the trade's risk can't be properly defined either
(Module 6). This is precisely one of the conditions this lesson lists
explicitly: "stop-loss location is unclear" is not a minor technicality
to work around — it's a specific, valid reason to pass on an otherwise
interesting-looking setup.

**No-Trade Checklist — a practical structure:**

- [ ] Setup's required conditions are not all present
- [ ] Risk amount and stop distance cannot both be clearly defined
- [ ] Major news creates conditions the plan's news rules exclude
- [ ] I have violated my own rules recently and haven't yet reviewed why
- [ ] Current market conditions don't match this setup's intended
      conditions
- [ ] Spread or execution conditions are unusually unfavorable right now
- [ ] I notice fear, greed, FOMO, revenge, or impatience currently
      present (Module 7)

If any box is checked, the plan calls for passing on the trade.

**"No trade" is a valid decision.** Choosing not to trade, when a
no-trade condition applies, is not a missed opportunity or a failure of
nerve — it's the plan working exactly as designed. This is not framed as
guaranteed protection against every possible loss; declining an
individual trade under a no-trade condition doesn't guarantee that
trade would have lost, and it doesn't guarantee overall results — it
simply means the decision was made deliberately, using the plan, rather
than by default.

> **Beginner mistake:** treating no-trade conditions as a soft suggestion
> rather than a rule with the same weight as an entry rule — a plan that
> only defines what to do, and never what to avoid, is missing half of
> what a complete plan actually is.

### Lesson 13 — Testing and Improving Your Plan

A plan is not automatically good simply because it's written down —
this lesson covers how to evaluate and refine one, without yet teaching
the full backtesting methodology Module 9 covers in depth.

**Why rules need to be tested, not just written:** a rule can be clearly
written and still turn out to be poorly calibrated — a setup defined too
narrowly to occur often enough to be useful, or too loosely to actually
filter out weak trades. Writing a rule down makes it *checkable*; it
doesn't automatically make it *correct*.

**Why clarity matters, and how to identify vague rules:** re-read each
section of your plan and ask, for every rule: could someone else, reading
only this sentence, apply it the same way you would? A rule that depends
on unstated judgment ("if it looks strong enough") fails this test, even
if it felt clear when it was written.

**Identifying conflicting rules:** occasionally, two rules in the same
plan pull in different directions — for example, an entry rule that
calls for immediate action on a trigger, paired with a news rule that
would have excluded that same window. Reviewing the whole plan together,
not just section by section, is how these conflicts surface.

**Scenario — discovering vague language in a written plan:** a trader
reviewing their own plan finds the entry rule: "enter when momentum looks
strong." Applying this lesson's test — could someone else apply this rule
identically? — the answer is no; "looks strong" depends entirely on the
reader's own judgment in the moment. Per Lesson 6, this gets rewritten
into an observable condition (a specific indicator threshold, a specific
structural confirmation) that any reader would apply the same way.

**Tracking rule adherence:** per Module 7, Lesson 14's Rule-Violation
Log, keeping a record of when written rules were and weren't actually
followed is what turns "I think I mostly follow my plan" into an honest,
specific answer.

**Scenario — tempted to change rules after several losing trades:** a
trader's plan has produced three losing trades in a row, all of which
followed the written rules correctly (a normal outcome, per Module 6,
Lesson 9). The trader feels a strong urge to change the setup definition
or loosen the entry criteria right now, to "fix" whatever seems to be
going wrong.

**Working through the decision:** per Module 7, Lesson 13, a short
losing streak is not, by itself, evidence that a plan's rules are the
problem — it's exactly the kind of small sample Module 9 will teach you
not to draw firm conclusions from. **Making controlled changes** means
adjusting a specific, identified issue (a genuinely vague rule, a
genuinely conflicting one) based on actual evidence gathered over a
meaningful sample — not reacting to three trades by rewriting the plan
from a place of frustration. **Avoiding constant strategy hopping** is
the direct discipline this scenario calls for: change deliberately,
rarely, and for a specific reason, not reflexively after every
uncomfortable stretch.

**Separating a bad outcome from a bad decision, applied to the plan
itself:** exactly as Module 7, Lesson 1 distinguished a losing trade from
a bad decision, a losing stretch and a bad plan are not the same thing
either. A properly followed plan that loses some trades is doing exactly
what Module 6's expectancy concept predicts it will do some percentage
of the time.

**Improving based on evidence:** genuine plan improvement comes from
Module 9's proper backtesting and journaling process — reviewing a
meaningful sample of trades, identifying a specific, recurring issue, and
making one deliberate, tracked change at a time. This lesson introduces
the *mindset* that a plan requires this kind of testing; Module 9 teaches
the *methodology* in full.

> **Beginner mistake:** treating a short losing stretch as proof the plan
> needs to change immediately, rather than distinguishing a genuinely
> vague or conflicting rule (worth fixing right away) from ordinary
> variance in outcomes (not evidence of anything on its own).

   structure.

8. Write your news rules per Lesson 9.
9. Build your before/during/after schedule per Lesson 10.
10. Write your no-trade conditions using Lesson 11's checklist as a
    starting structure.
11. Write your psychology/behavior rules, drawing on Module 7, Lesson 14.

14. Run the Final Trading Plan Audit below.

**Final Trading Plan Audit:**

- [ ] Are my markets clearly defined?
- [ ] Are my timeframes defined?
- [ ] Is my setup objective — stated in observable terms, not feelings?
- [ ] Are my entry rules clear?
- [ ] Is my stop-loss logic clear?
- [ ] Are my exit rules clear?

> **Beginner mistake:** treating a completed plan as a finished product
> rather than a first draft — the plan you write in this module is meant
> to be tested and refined, per Lesson 13 and Module 9, not archived as a
> permanent, unquestionable document.

## Key Takeaways

- A trading plan turns intentions into rules — specific enough to follow
  consistently and check afterward, unlike an idea that only exists in a
  trader's head.
- A strategy is the market-reading logic; a complete trading plan
  includes the strategy plus risk, schedule, news, no-trade, and
  psychology rules built around it.
- No single trading style, market list, or timeframe combination is
  universally correct — the right choice matches a trader's own time,
  schedule, and experience.
- A setup should be defined in observable terms, with required
  conditions clearly separated from optional confluences.
- Risk rules belong inside the written plan, decided before trading
  begins — this module doesn't repeat Module 6's calculations, only where
  they live inside the plan.
- No-trade conditions are as important as entry conditions; declining a
  trade under a written rule is the plan working as intended, not a
  missed opportunity.
- A complete plan assembles fourteen components into one document a
  trader could hand to someone else and have them understand exactly how
  the trader trades.
- A written plan is a first draft, not a proven system — it needs
  testing, tracked adherence, and evidence-based improvement, which
  Module 9 teaches in full.
- Nothing in this module claims a written plan guarantees profitable
  trading — a plan controls how decisions get made, not what the market
  does next.
