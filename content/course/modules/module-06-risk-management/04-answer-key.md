# Module 6 — Answer Key

Kept in a separate file from `03-quiz.md` on purpose, so the quiz can be
worked through honestly before checking. Question numbers match exactly.

**1. Answer: B) The set of decisions and calculations that control how
much capital is exposed to loss on a trade or across an account.** Risk
management doesn't guarantee profitability (ruling out A) or eliminate
losses entirely (ruling out C), and it matters for every account size, not
only large ones (ruling out D) (Lesson 1).

**2. Answer: account size → risk % → money at risk → stop-loss distance
→ position size.** Each step feeds the next: the account size and a
chosen risk percentage determine the money at risk, which combined with
the stop-loss distance determines the resulting position size (Lesson
1).

**3. Answer: $50.** $4,000 × 0.0125 = $50 (Lesson 2).

**4. Answer: False.** Lesson 2 is explicit that the chosen risk
percentage is a decision, not a universal rule — 0.5%, 1%, and 2% are all
commonly used starting points, and none is prescribed as the only
acceptable approach (Lesson 2).

**5. Answer: False.** Per Lesson 3, in fast-moving markets (major news, a
weekend gap), price can move past a stop-loss level before the order
fills, resulting in slippage — execution at the next available price
rather than necessarily the exact requested price (Lesson 3).

**6. Answer guide.** Any three of: the currency pair, position size,
account currency, and the current exchange rate (Lesson 4).

**7. Answer: dollar risk = $18; position size = 0.60 mini lots.** $1,800
× 0.01 = $18. $18 ÷ (30 pips × $1/pip) = $18 ÷ $30 = 0.60 (Lesson 5).

**8. Answer: 1:3.** $75 ÷ $25 = 3, written as a 1:3 ratio (Lesson 6).

**9. Answer: approximately 28.57%.** Breakeven win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + 2.5) =
1 ÷ 3.5 = 0.2857 ≈ 28.57% (Lesson 6).

**10. Answer: $7.50.** Expectancy = (0.50 × $35) − (0.50 × $20) = $17.50
− $10.00 = $7.50 per trade (Lesson 7).

**11. Answer: False.** Lesson 7's third worked example shows a 70% win
rate with a small average win and a much larger average loss producing a
*negative* expectancy — win rate alone says nothing about profitability
without also knowing the average size of wins and losses (Lesson 7).

**12. Answer: approximately 66.67%.** Remaining balance after a 40% loss
= 60%. Required recovery = (1 ÷ 0.60) − 1 = 1.6667 − 1 = 0.6667 ≈ 66.67%
(Lesson 8).

**13. Answer: Loss 1: $4,000 − $40.00 = $3,960.00. Loss 2: $3,960.00 −
$39.60 = $3,920.40. Loss 3: $3,920.40 − $39.20 = $3,881.20. Total
drawdown ≈ 2.97%.** Each loss is 1% of the balance immediately before it:
$4,000 × 0.01 = $40.00; $3,960.00 × 0.01 = $39.60; $3,920.40 × 0.01 =
$39.20. ($4,000 − $3,881.20) ÷ $4,000 = 2.97% (Lesson 9).

**14. Answer: False.** Per Lesson 9, a losing streak is a statistically
normal outcome for many strategies with genuinely positive expectancy —
it does not, on its own, prove a strategy is broken, and reacting by
increasing risk, revenge trading, or abandoning all rules tends to cause
more damage than the streak itself (Lesson 9).

**15. Answer guide.** **Leverage** is a ratio describing how much larger
a position you can control relative to the capital you put up. **Margin**
is the actual capital required to open and hold a specific position at
that leverage. **Risk** is the amount you could actually lose on the
trade, set by position size and stop-loss distance — not by leverage or
margin at all (Lesson 10).

**16. Answer: notional value = $250,000; margin required = $5,000.**
200,000 × 1.25000 = $250,000. $250,000 ÷ 50 = $5,000 (Lesson 10).

**17. Answer guide.** Any two of: slippage (execution at a different
price than requested during fast-moving markets), the spread (a cost
paid at entry), and commission or other broker fees (Lesson 3).
