# Module 5 — Answer Key

Kept in a separate file from `03-quiz.md` on purpose, so the quiz can be
worked through honestly before checking. Question numbers match exactly.

**1. Answer: B) The study of the economic and political conditions that
influence a currency's value, used alongside technical analysis and price
action.** Fundamental analysis doesn't replace technical analysis or
price action (ruling out A); it doesn't predict an exact future price
(ruling out C); and it's used by traders across timeframes, not only
long-term investors (ruling out D) (Lesson 1).

**2. Answer guide.** **Previous** is the value from the last time this
report was released. **Forecast** (or "expected"/"consensus") is what
economists and analysts predicted, on average, ahead of the release.
**Actual** is the real value once the report is published (Lesson 2).

**3. Answer: surprise = +0.4 percentage points above forecast; change =
+0.5 percentage points above the previous reading.** 2.9% − 2.5% = 0.4.
2.9% − 2.4% = 0.5. This is both an upside surprise and an acceleration
compared to last month (Lesson 2).

**4. Answer: False.** It's the reverse — a report matching its forecast
is largely already "priced in" and tends to produce a comparatively small
reaction, while a genuine surprise relative to forecast is what tends to
move price more (Lesson 2).

**5. Answer guide.** Any one of: a rate move that was already fully
expected is largely priced in before the announcement and may barely
move price; a rate decision paired with guidance that sounds more
cautious than expected can coincide with the currency weakening even on
a hike; and markets often move more on the *expected future path* of
rates than on the current rate itself (Lesson 3).

**6. Answer: B) Excludes food and energy prices, aiming to show the more
persistent underlying trend.** Core CPI isn't limited to housing (ruling
out A), it's calculated within the same currency's economy, not a
different one (ruling out C), and it isn't always higher than headline —
either can be higher depending on what's happening in food and energy
prices at the time (ruling out D) (Lesson 4).

**7. Answer: surprise = −0.2 percentage points below forecast; generally
read as a stronger labor-market signal.** 4.0% − 4.2% = −0.2. A lower
unemployment rate than expected is generally read as a positive surprise
for the labor market (Lesson 5).

**8. Answer: actual − forecast = −40,000; actual − previous =
−30,000.** 160,000 − 200,000 = −40,000. 160,000 − 190,000 = −30,000. A
weaker labor-market report than both expected and the prior month
(Lesson 6).

**9. Answer: False.** A strong NFP report doesn't guarantee currency
strength — the actual reaction depends on how it compares to what was
priced in, what the unemployment rate and wage data released alongside it
show, other data due the same week, and existing market positioning
(Lesson 6).

**10. Answer: −0.5 percentage points below forecast.** 1.7% − 2.2% =
−0.5 (Lesson 7).

**11. Answer guide.** Any one of: GDP is reported less frequently than
inflation or employment data (typically quarterly); it's often revised
more than once as more complete data arrives; and it says nothing on its
own about how growth is distributed across the economy or about
forward-looking conditions (Lesson 7).

**12. Answer guide.** Any two of: the written policy statement
accompanying a decision, the press conference (where a spokesperson
elaborates and answers questions), and forward guidance about the bank's
likely future path (Lesson 8).

**13. Answer: this is a downside surprise relative to forecast (2.6% vs.
2.9% forecast), and inflation is decelerating compared to the previous
reading (2.6% vs. 2.8% previous).** Both comparisons — actual vs.
forecast, and actual vs. previous — point the same direction here: cooler
than expected, and cooler than last time (Lesson 9, applying Lesson 2's
framework).

**14. Answer: False.** Lesson 11 is explicit that the same broad type of
geopolitical event has produced different, sometimes opposite, market
reactions in different situations, depending on the countries involved,
what the market already expected, and broader conditions at the time
(Lesson 11).

**15. Answer guide.** No — the fundamental backdrop (upside inflation and
employment surprises, more cautious central-bank guidance) and the
technical picture (bearish structure, no recent bullish BOS) point in
different directions here. Per Lesson 12, this is a genuine disagreement
worth taking seriously, not a contradiction to explain away — a trader
following this module's framework would treat it as a reason for added
caution rather than choosing whichever picture happens to support a trade
they already wanted to take, the same discipline Module 4 taught for
conflicting timeframes (Lesson 12).
