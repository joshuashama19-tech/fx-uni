# Module 1 — Forex Fundamentals

*"Understanding the Market Before You Trade It"*

## Module Introduction

Every trader who ever became consistent started in the same place you're
starting now: not knowing what half the words meant. "Pip," "spread,"
"lot," "leverage" — none of it is complicated once it's explained
properly, but almost nobody explains it properly. Most beginners piece
their understanding together from scattered YouTube videos, contradictory
forum posts, and broker marketing pages that have every reason to make
trading sound simpler and safer than it is. The result is a trader who
can repeat the vocabulary without actually understanding what any of it
means when real money and real risk are on the line.

This module fixes that, properly, once. By the end of it, you won't just
recognize the terms — you'll understand what's actually happening in the
market when you open a chart, place an order, or watch a price move.
That understanding is the floor everything else in this course is built
on. Modules 2 through 5 teach you to read charts and analyze the market;
Modules 6 and 7 teach you to manage risk and your own psychology; Modules
8 through 10 turn all of it into a repeatable process. None of that works
if the foundation in this module is shaky.

Take your time here. There's no reward for rushing Module 1 — every
later module assumes you're fluent in what's covered here, and going
back to re-read a section now costs you a few minutes. Going back after
you've already lost money because a term was fuzzy costs a lot more.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Explain what the Forex market is and why it exists.
- Identify the base currency and quote currency in any currency pair.
- Classify a currency pair as a major, minor, or exotic.
- Read a bid and ask price and calculate the spread between them.
- Calculate how many pips a price has moved, including for JPY pairs.
- Explain what a lot is and calculate the approximate value of a pip for
  different lot sizes.
- Distinguish between market orders, limit orders, and stop orders, and
  identify which one fits a given scenario.
- Explain what leverage and margin are, and calculate margin required for
  a simple position.
- Identify the four major trading sessions and explain why session
  overlaps matter.
- List the main types of participants in the Forex market.
- Explain what a broker actually does and what "regulated" means in
  practice.
- Walk through, step by step, what happens when a trade is opened and
  closed.
- Recognize the most common mistakes beginners make before they've even
  placed a trade.

### Lesson 1 — What Is Forex?

Think about the last time you traveled to another country, or bought
something priced in a currency that wasn't your own. Somewhere in that
transaction, one currency was exchanged for another at a specific rate.
Multiply that exact same idea by millions of transactions a day — tourists,
importers, exporters, governments, investors, and banks all exchanging
one currency for another — and you have the Forex market.

"Forex" is short for **foreign exchange**: the global market where
currencies are traded against each other. There's no single building or
exchange where this happens — unlike a stock exchange, Forex is
**decentralized**. It's a network of banks, financial institutions,
brokers, and traders connected electronically across the world, which is
part of why it operates nearly 24 hours a day during the trading week
(more on that in Lesson 10).

**Why does it matter to you?** Every price you'll ever look at in this
course — EUR/USD, GBP/JPY, USD/CAD — is a real, functioning market with
genuine buyers and sellers, driven by genuine economic activity: trade
between countries, interest rate decisions, investment flows, and
day-to-day commerce. It is not a game or a simulation. Understanding that
the price on your screen reflects real supply and demand, not a random
number generator, is the first mental shift toward trading it responsibly.

**How it works, in one sentence:** you're always trading one currency
*for* another — you're never just "buying dollars," you're buying dollars
*with* something else, which is exactly what Lesson 3 covers in detail.

> **Beginner mistake:** treating Forex as fundamentally different from
> "real" markets, or assuming that because it's accessible from a phone
> app, it's simpler or lower-stakes than it is. The accessibility is real.
> The risk is too.

### Lesson 2 — How the Forex Market Works

Because Forex has no central exchange, trades happen through a layered
network instead. At the top, large banks trade enormous volumes directly
with each other — this is called the **interbank market**. Below that
are smaller banks, financial institutions, hedge funds, and eventually
brokers, who aggregate access for individual traders like you.

When you place a trade through a retail broker, you're not shouting an
order across a trading floor — you're sending an electronic order that
the broker fills, typically by matching it against their own pricing feed
or passing it through to a liquidity provider. The price you see updating
on your chart is a live reflection of buying and selling pressure moving
through that network.

**Why does it matter?** Two things follow directly from this structure.
First, prices move continuously and can move quickly, because so many
participants are active at once across the world. Second, the price you
get isn't set by one authority — it's an aggregate of what the market is
actually willing to pay, which is why two brokers can show you very
slightly different prices for the same pair at the same moment (the
difference is usually tiny, but it exists).

**What it looks like in practice:** open any live EUR/USD chart and watch
it for sixty seconds. The price ticks up and down constantly, sometimes
by a fraction of a cent, sometimes by more. That constant movement is the
market processing new information — a trade, an order, a piece of news —
in real time. Nothing about that movement is guaranteed to continue in
any particular direction just because it moved that way a moment ago.

> **Beginner mistake:** assuming that because a price is moving, it must
> be about to "break out" in that direction. Short-term price movement is
> extremely common and, on its own, tells you very little. Module 3 and
> Module 4 teach you how to actually interpret it.

### Lesson 3 — Understanding Currency Pairs

Because you're always exchanging one currency for another, Forex prices
are always quoted as a **pair** — two currencies, written together.

Take **EUR/USD**. The first currency listed (EUR) is called the **base
currency**. The second currency (USD) is called the **quote currency**.
The price tells you how much of the quote currency it takes to buy one
unit of the base currency.

**Example:** If EUR/USD is trading at **1.08500**, that means 1 Euro is
worth 1.08500 US Dollars. If the price rises to 1.09000, the Euro has
gotten *stronger* relative to the Dollar — it now takes more Dollars to
buy the same Euro. If the price falls to 1.08000, the Euro has gotten
*weaker* relative to the Dollar.

This is the single most important mental model in this lesson: **a
currency pair's price is a comparison, not a standalone value.** You're
never just betting "the Euro will go up" in isolation — you're betting
the Euro will strengthen *specifically against the Dollar*. The same
Euro could be strengthening against the Dollar while weakening against
the British Pound at the very same moment, because those are two
completely different comparisons.

**What it looks like in a trade:** if you believe the Euro will
strengthen against the US Dollar, you would **buy** EUR/USD. If you
believe the Euro will weaken against the US Dollar, you would **sell**
EUR/USD. Either direction is a normal, tradeable decision — Forex isn't
only about prices going up.

> **Beginner mistake:** mixing up which currency is the base and which is
> the quote, especially with less familiar pairs. A simple habit fixes
> this: the base currency is always the one written *first*, and it's
> always the one you're figuratively "holding" when you buy the pair.

### Lesson 4 — Major, Minor and Exotic Pairs

Not all currency pairs are equally active, and grouping them helps you
understand what you're looking at before you ever open a chart.

**Major pairs** always include the US Dollar paired with one of the
world's other most-traded currencies: EUR/USD, GBP/USD, USD/JPY,
USD/CHF, USD/CAD, AUD/USD, and NZD/USD. These pairs typically have the
highest trading volume, which generally means tighter spreads (more on
spreads in Lesson 5) and more consistent price behavior.

**Minor pairs** (sometimes called "crosses") pair two major currencies
that don't include the US Dollar — for example EUR/GBP, GBP/JPY, or
AUD/NZD. They're still actively traded, just with somewhat lower volume
than the majors.

**Exotic pairs** pair a major currency with the currency of a smaller or
developing economy — for example USD/TRY (Turkish Lira), USD/ZAR (South
African Rand), or USD/MXN (Mexican Peso). Exotics typically have wider
spreads and can move more unpredictably around local economic or
political events.

**Why does it matter?** As a beginner, the majors are the sensible place
to start. They're the most liquid (meaning there's the most active buying
and selling), which generally means more predictable spreads and fewer
sudden price gaps from low trading activity. This course uses major
pairs in almost every example for exactly that reason.

| Category | Example pairs | Typical characteristic |
|---|---|---|
| Major | EUR/USD, GBP/USD, USD/JPY | Highest volume, tightest typical spreads |
| Minor | EUR/GBP, AUD/NZD, GBP/JPY | Actively traded, moderate spreads |
| Exotic | USD/TRY, USD/ZAR, USD/MXN | Lower liquidity, wider spreads, more volatility |

> **Beginner mistake:** jumping straight into exotic pairs because they
> appear to "move more," without understanding that wider spreads and
> less predictable liquidity make them harder to manage — not easier to
> profit from.

### Lesson 5 — Bid, Ask and Spread

Every tradeable price you see is actually two prices, not one.

The **bid** is the price at which you can *sell* the base currency right
now. The **ask** (sometimes called the "offer") is the price at which you
can *buy* the base currency right now. The ask is always slightly higher
than the bid.

The difference between them is the **spread**, and it's one of the ways
brokers are compensated for providing access to the market.

**Example:** Suppose EUR/USD shows a bid of **1.08498** and an ask of
**1.08512**. The spread is 1.08512 − 1.08498 = **0.00014**, or (as you'll
learn to express it in Lesson 6) **1.4 pips**.

If you buy at the ask (1.08512), your position is immediately showing a
small loss equal to the spread, because if you tried to close it
instantly, you'd be selling back at the lower bid price. This isn't a
trick or a flaw — it's simply the mechanical cost of entering a trade,
and it's why a trade needs to move slightly in your favor before it
reaches breakeven.

**Why does it matter?** The spread is a real, guaranteed cost on every
single trade you place, regardless of whether the trade ultimately wins
or loses. On major pairs during active trading hours, spreads are
typically small. On exotic pairs, or during low-liquidity periods (like
right around a major holiday), spreads can widen noticeably — sometimes
dramatically.

> **Beginner mistake:** ignoring the spread when judging whether a trade
> "worked." A trade that closes a few pips above your entry might still
> be a net loss once the spread is accounted for.

### Lesson 6 — Pips and Pipettes

A **pip** ("percentage in point") is the standard unit used to measure
how much a currency pair's price has moved. For most pairs, a pip is the
fourth decimal place. For pairs involving the Japanese Yen, because of
how the Yen is priced, a pip is the *second* decimal place instead.

**Example (non-JPY pair):** EUR/USD moves from **1.08500** to
**1.08550**. That's a move of 0.00050, or **5 pips**.

**Example (JPY pair):** USD/JPY moves from **149.500** to **149.750**.
That's a move of 0.250, or **25 pips**.

Many brokers now quote prices with one extra decimal place beyond the
standard pip — this smaller unit is called a **pipette**, equal to
one-tenth of a pip. So EUR/USD at **1.08505** is showing "half a pip"
more precisely: 0.5 pips, or 5 pipettes, above 1.08500.

**Why does it matter?** Pips are the common language for describing
price movement, profit and loss, and risk — regardless of which currency
pair you're trading or which country you're in. When another trader says
"I took 20 pips on that trade" or "my stop loss is 15 pips away," pips
are what makes that instantly comparable, even though 20 pips means a
very different dollar amount depending on the pair and position size
(that connection is covered fully in Module 6).

| Pair | From | To | Pip movement |
|---|---|---|---|
| EUR/USD | 1.08500 | 1.08550 | 5 pips |
| GBP/USD | 1.26300 | 1.26200 | −10 pips |
| USD/JPY | 149.500 | 149.750 | 25 pips |

> **Beginner mistake:** applying the same "4th decimal place" rule to JPY
> pairs and being off by a factor of 100 when estimating a move. Always
> check which decimal place counts as a pip for the specific pair you're
> looking at.

### Lesson 7 — Lot Sizes

A **lot** is the standardized unit of trade size in Forex — it tells you
how much of the base currency your position actually represents.

- A **standard lot** = 100,000 units of the base currency.
- A **mini lot** = 10,000 units (one-tenth of a standard lot).
- A **micro lot** = 1,000 units (one-hundredth of a standard lot).

**Why does it matter?** Lot size directly determines how much money one
pip of movement is worth to you. That connection is the entire basis of
position sizing, which you'll study properly in Module 6 — but you need
to understand the mechanics here first.

**Example:** For a pair where the quote currency is USD (like EUR/USD),
one pip on a standard lot is worth approximately **$10**. On a mini lot,
approximately **$1**. On a micro lot, approximately **$0.10**. (These are
approximate because the exact value depends on the current exchange
rate, but they're close enough to reason with as a beginner.)

| Lot size | Units | Approx. value per pip (USD-quoted pair) |
|---|---|---|
| Standard lot | 100,000 | ≈ $10.00 |
| Mini lot | 10,000 | ≈ $1.00 |
| Micro lot | 1,000 | ≈ $0.10 |

Pip value also **scales directly with position size** — you don't need a
separate table for every possible size, just scale the nearest tier up
or down. Half a mini lot (5,000 units) is worth half the mini-lot pip
value, roughly $0.50 per pip. Two standard lots is worth twice the
standard-lot pip value, roughly $20 per pip. The relationship is linear:
double the units, double the pip value; halve the units, halve it.

So a 20-pip move on a standard lot is roughly a $200 difference. The
exact same 20-pip move on a micro lot is roughly a $2 difference. Neither
number is inherently "right" — the right lot size depends entirely on
your account size and how much you've decided to risk, which is exactly
what Module 6 teaches you to calculate deliberately instead of guessing.

**Connecting back to spread (Lesson 5):** now that you know what a pip
is worth, the spread from Lesson 5 stops being abstract. That EUR/USD
quote with a 1.4-pip spread would represent about 1.4 × $10 ≈ **$14** of
spread cost on a standard lot, using the simplified pip-value example
used in this module — paid the moment the trade opens, before it's had
any chance to move in your favor. The exact dollar figure varies with
the pair, the lot size, and your account's currency, but the mechanic is
the same on every trade: the spread is a real, immediate cost, and now
you can actually put a number on it.

> **Beginner mistake:** choosing a lot size because it "feels" like a
> reasonable number of units, without calculating what that size actually
> means in real money terms relative to the account. This is one of the
> fastest ways a beginner takes on far more risk than they realized.

### Lesson 8 — Market, Limit and Stop Orders

An **order** is simply an instruction to buy or sell. There are three
basic types you need to know before you place your first trade.

**Market order.** Executes immediately at the current available price.
Use this when you want to enter or exit *right now*, at whatever the
market is currently offering.

**Limit order.** Executes only if the price reaches a *specific, more
favorable* level you choose in advance. A **buy limit** is placed below
the current price (you're waiting to buy cheaper). A **sell limit** is
placed above the current price (you're waiting to sell higher).

**Stop order.** Executes only if the price reaches a *specific, less
favorable* level you choose in advance — used to enter a breakout, or,
most commonly, to automatically exit a losing trade (a "stop loss").
A **buy stop** is placed above the current price. A **sell stop** is
placed below the current price.

**Example scenario:** EUR/USD is trading at 1.08500. You believe that if
price drops to 1.08300, it's likely to bounce back up, so you place a
**buy limit at 1.08300** — you're instructing your platform to buy only
if price falls to that more favorable level, without you having to watch
the screen. Separately, you also believe that if price breaks *above*
1.08700, it's likely to keep climbing, so you place a **buy stop at
1.08700** on a different setup — instructing your platform to buy only if
price rises to that level, confirming the breakout.

**Example scenario (protecting an open trade):** Now suppose you're
already in a long EUR/USD position, bought at 1.08500. To cap your loss
if the trade goes wrong, you place a **sell stop at 1.08400** — 10 pips
below your entry — which automatically closes the trade if price falls
that far. This is a **protective stop loss**, and mechanically it's a
sell stop order: it sits below the current price and triggers on a
downward move, exactly like the breakout-entry stop above, just used to
exit instead of enter. Separately, if you wanted to sell only at a more
favorable, *higher* price instead — say you expect price to struggle to
climb past 1.08650 — you'd place a **sell limit at 1.08650** rather than
a market or stop order. (Why a price level might be hard to climb past
in the first place is exactly what Module 2's support and resistance
lessons cover.)

| Order type | Executes at | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Market order | Current price, immediately | Enter or exit right now |
| Buy limit | Below current price | Buy at a more favorable (lower) price |
| Sell limit | Above current price | Sell at a more favorable (higher) price |
| Buy stop | Above current price | Enter a breakout upward |
| Sell stop | Below current price | Enter a breakout downward, or exit a long trade (stop loss) |

> **Beginner mistake:** using a market order out of impatience when a
> limit order would have gotten a better price, or forgetting to set a
> stop order at all — leaving a trade with no automatic exit if the
> market moves against it.

### Lesson 9 — Leverage and Margin

**Leverage** lets you control a larger position size than the cash you
actually have to deposit for it. It's typically expressed as a ratio,
like 30:1 or 100:1.

**Margin** is the amount of your own money your broker actually sets
aside — "locks up" — to open and maintain a leveraged position. It's not
a fee; it's collateral that gets released back to you when the position
closes.

**Example:** Suppose your broker offers 30:1 leverage, and you want to
open one standard lot of EUR/USD (100,000 units) at a price of 1.08500.
The full **notional value** of that position — the full market value of
the position, before leverage — is 100,000 × 1.08500 = **$108,500**.
With 30:1 leverage, the margin required is roughly $108,500 ÷ 30 ≈
**$3,617**. You don't need $108,500 in your account — you need
approximately $3,617 set aside as margin. The rest is the leverage your
broker is extending.

> **Risk note:** leverage does not just change how much capital you
> need — it changes how much your account moves for a given price move,
> in both directions. In the example above, if EUR/USD moves 50 pips
> against you, that's roughly a $500 loss (using the standard-lot pip
> value from Lesson 7) — against margin of about $3,617, that's already a
> meaningful percentage of the capital you set aside for that one trade.
> Leverage magnifies gains and losses equally. It does not make a trade
> "safer" or "cheaper" — it makes the same price movement matter more to
> your account balance.

**Why does it matter?** Leverage is exactly why Module 6 (Risk
Management) exists as its own dedicated module. Understanding leverage
mechanically, as covered here, is not the same as understanding how to
size a position responsibly — that's a separate skill, built later in
this course, on top of what you're learning now.

> **Beginner mistake:** treating high leverage as "more buying power" in
> a purely positive sense, without registering that it also means a
> smaller adverse price move can represent a larger percentage loss of
> the capital actually committed to that trade.

### Lesson 10 — Forex Trading Sessions

Because Forex trades through a network of financial centers around the
world rather than one exchange, the market is effectively active nearly
24 hours a day across the trading week, moving through four broad
regional sessions:

- **Sydney session** — the trading day's opening session, generally
  lower volume. Roughly **22:00–07:00 UTC**.
- **Tokyo (Asian) session** — active trading in Asian markets, notable
  activity in Yen pairs. Roughly **00:00–09:00 UTC**.
- **London (European) session** — one of the highest-volume sessions
  globally. Roughly **08:00–17:00 UTC**.
- **New York (US) session** — the other highest-volume session,
  overlapping with the end of the London session. Roughly **13:00–22:00
  UTC**.

> **Note:** these hours are approximate, not fixed. Exact open and close
> times shift by about an hour depending on daylight-saving changes in
> each region — and different regions change their clocks on different
> dates — so treat these as a general guide to when each session is
> typically active, not a permanent schedule.

**Why does it matter?** Trading activity — and with it, typical spread
and price movement — isn't constant throughout the day. The **London/New
York overlap** (when both sessions are open simultaneously) is generally
the most active window for major pairs, because participants from both
regions are trading at once. Sessions with very little overlap, or
periods right around session close, can show noticeably lower activity
and, at times, wider spreads.

**What it looks like in practice:** a beginner watching EUR/USD during
the Sydney session alone might see it barely move for a stretch, then
watch it become noticeably more active once London opens. That's not
random — it's a direct result of which financial centers are actively
trading at that hour.

> **Beginner mistake:** trading (or expecting a strategy to behave
> consistently) during very low-liquidity hours without accounting for
> the fact that lower activity can mean wider spreads and less reliable
> price behavior.

### Lesson 11 — Who Participates in the Forex Market?

Understanding who's actually on the other side of the market helps
explain why prices move the way they do.

- **Central banks** — influence currency values through interest rate
  decisions and monetary policy (covered in depth in Module 5).
- **Commercial and investment banks** — the largest participants by
  volume, trading for clients and for themselves.
- **Corporations** — companies doing business internationally need to
  exchange currencies to pay suppliers, receive revenue, or **hedge**
  (reduce exposure to) risk.
- **Governments and institutions** — including sovereign wealth funds and
  large asset managers.
- **Retail traders** — individuals, like you, trading through a broker.
  Retail participation is a real and legitimate part of the market, but
  it represents a relatively small share of total volume compared to the
  institutional participants above.

**Why does it matter?** It's a useful reality check: the market isn't
moving because of what retail traders collectively expect it to do — it
moves primarily because of enormous flows tied to trade, investment, and
policy. That's part of why chasing "what everyone online thinks will
happen" is a weak basis for a trading decision compared to understanding
structure, analysis, and risk — the actual subject of the rest of this
course.

> **Beginner mistake:** assuming retail sentiment (forums, social media,
> influencers) meaningfully drives major currency pairs the way it
> sometimes can in smaller, less liquid markets.

### Lesson 12 — Understanding Brokers

A **broker** is the platform that gives you access to the market. When
you place a trade, your broker is the entity that executes it — either by
matching it internally, passing it to a liquidity provider, or some
combination of both, depending on their model.

**Regulation** matters because it determines what rules a broker actually
has to follow — things like keeping client funds in segregated accounts,
meeting minimum capital requirements, and being accountable to a
financial authority if something goes wrong. A broker regulated by a
recognized financial authority in a reputable jurisdiction is operating
under real oversight. An unregulated broker, or one only licensed in a
jurisdiction with minimal oversight, is not offering you the same
protection — regardless of how professional their website looks.

**Why does it matter?** Your broker isn't just a neutral app — it's the
**counterparty** (directly or indirectly) — the other side of your
trade — to every trade you place, and the custodian of the money in your
account. Checking a broker's regulatory
status, where they hold client funds, and their actual trading
conditions (spreads, execution speed, available pairs) is a practical
safety step, not an optional one.

> **Note:** this course does not recommend or endorse any specific
> broker. Module 10 covers broker-safety checks in more depth once you
> have the analytical foundation to evaluate trading conditions
> meaningfully.

> **Beginner mistake:** choosing a broker based only on bonus offers or
> marketing, without checking regulatory status or reading the actual
> account terms.

### Lesson 13 — Basic Trade Execution

Let's put the whole module together by walking through a simple trade
from start to finish, step by step.

**Scenario:** A trader is watching EUR/USD, currently showing a bid of
1.08493 and an ask of 1.08507 (a spread of about 1.4 pips). Based on
their analysis (the kind you'll learn to do properly starting in Module
2), they decide to buy.

1. **They place a buy market order.** It fills at the ask price,
   1.08507 — because buying always executes at the ask.
2. **They set a stop loss at 1.08407 — mechanically a sell stop order**,
   10 pips below their entry, per Lesson 8. It's their predetermined
   maximum acceptable loss on this trade if it moves against them (why 10
   pips specifically, and how that connects to position sizing, is
   covered fully in Module 6).
3. **They set a take profit at 1.08707**, 20 pips above their entry —
   their planned exit if the trade moves in their favor.
4. **Price moves in their favor**, reaching 1.08707. The take profit
   order executes automatically, closing the trade.
5. **The trade closes at a profit of 20 pips**, before accounting for the
   spread paid on entry — which, as Lesson 5 explained, is already
   reflected in the fact that they bought at the higher ask price rather
   than the mid-price.

This is the same basic sequence behind essentially every trade you'll
ever place: analyze, enter with an order type that fits the plan, define
risk with a stop loss, define a target (or a rule for managing the trade)
with a take profit or a clear exit plan, and let the order execute.

> **Beginner mistake:** entering a trade without steps 2 and 3 already
> decided *before* placing it — deciding where to exit only after
> watching the trade move, which is exactly the situation psychology
> (Module 7) makes hardest to handle well.

## Common Beginner Mistakes (Module Recap)

Gathered here in one place for quick reference — each is explained fully
in its related lesson above:

1. Assuming the market's accessibility makes it lower-risk than it is
   (Lesson 1).
2. Reading short-term price movement as a reliable signal on its own
   (Lesson 2).
3. Confusing the base and quote currency in a pair (Lesson 3).
4. Trading exotic pairs early because they "move more," without
   accounting for wider spreads and lower liquidity (Lesson 4).
5. Ignoring the spread when judging whether a trade actually worked
   (Lesson 5).
6. Misreading pip movement on JPY pairs by applying the non-JPY decimal
   rule (Lesson 6).
7. Picking a lot size without calculating its real money value first
   (Lesson 7).
8. Placing a trade with no stop order at all (Lesson 8).
9. Treating leverage as only "more buying power" without registering the
   added risk (Lesson 9).
10. Trading actively during very low-liquidity hours without adjusting
    expectations (Lesson 10).
11. Overweighting retail sentiment as a driver of major pairs (Lesson
    11).
12. Choosing a broker based on marketing instead of regulation and terms
    (Lesson 12).
13. Entering a trade before deciding the exit (Lesson 13).

## Practical Examples

**Example A — Reading a full quote.** A trader looks at their platform
and sees: `GBP/USD  1.26340 / 1.26355`. Working through it: the base
currency is GBP, the quote currency is USD; the bid is 1.26340, the ask
is 1.26355; the spread is 1.26355 − 1.26340 = 0.00015, or **1.5 pips**.
GBP/USD is a **major pair**. If the trader buys, they pay the ask,
1.26355; if they sell, they receive the bid, 1.26340.

**Example B — Sizing a trade the wrong way, then the right way.** A
trader with a $2,000 account decides, purely by feel, to trade one
standard lot of EUR/USD because "it doesn't seem like that much." Using
Lesson 7, one standard lot is worth roughly $10 per pip. A routine 30-pip
adverse move — nothing unusual — would cost roughly $300, or **15% of
the entire account**, on a single trade. Recognizing this (this is
exactly the kind of check Module 6 formalizes), the trader instead
chooses a micro lot (0.01 lots), worth roughly $0.10 per pip — the same
30-pip move now costs about $3, or 0.15% of the account. Same market
move, completely different outcome, based entirely on a decision made
*before* the trade — position size.

**Example C — Choosing an order type for a scenario.** USD/JPY is
trading at 149.500. A trader believes price is likely to dip to 149.200
before continuing higher, and wants to buy only if that happens, without
watching the screen all day. The correct tool is a **buy limit order at
149.200** — not a market order (which would enter immediately at the
current, higher price) and not a buy stop (which would only trigger on
a move *upward* through a level, the opposite of what they expect).

## Key Takeaways

- Forex is the decentralized global market for exchanging currencies,
  driven by genuine economic activity, not a game or simulation.
- Every price is a comparison between two currencies — the base and the
  quote — never a standalone value.
- Pairs are grouped into majors, minors, and exotics, mainly by liquidity
  and typical spread.
- Every quote has a bid and an ask; the difference is the spread, a real
  cost on every trade.
- A pip measures price movement; JPY pairs use a different decimal place
  than other pairs.
- Lot size determines how much money each pip of movement is actually
  worth.
- Market, limit, and stop orders each serve a different purpose — knowing
  which to use, and when, is a practical skill, not trivia.
- Leverage lets you control a larger position with less capital, but it
  magnifies both gains and losses equally — it doesn't reduce risk.
- Trading activity varies by session, and the London/New York overlap is
  typically the most active window for major pairs.
- The market is driven mainly by large institutional flows, not retail
  sentiment.
- Your broker's regulation and terms are a real, practical safety
  consideration — not a formality.
- Every trade follows the same basic sequence: analyze, enter, define
  risk, define an exit plan.
