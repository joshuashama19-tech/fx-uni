# Module 7 — Answer Key

Kept in a separate file from `03-quiz.md` on purpose, so the quiz can be
worked through honestly before checking. Question numbers match exactly.

**1. Answer: B) The study of how emotion, habit, and decision-making
affect whether a trader actually follows their own process.** This
module does not claim emotion can be eliminated (ruling out A), does not
guarantee profitability (ruling out C), and does not replace Module 6's
calculations — it covers the behavior around them (ruling out D)
(Lesson 1).

**2. Answer guide.** A losing trade is one where the process was followed
correctly (valid setup, proper sizing, a logical stop) and the trade
simply didn't work out — a normal, expected outcome some percentage of
the time. A bad decision is abandoning the trader's own criteria,
sizing, or rules, regardless of whether that specific decision happens to
win or lose. Yes — a trade can follow the process completely and still
lose money; that is not a mistake (Lesson 1).

**3. Answer: avoiding a valid setup after a losing streak.** The setup
met every stated criterion, and the hesitation wasn't based on anything
specific to this setup — it was driven by how the recent losses felt
(Lesson 2).

**4. Answer: moving take profit because a trade is winning.** The target
was changed based on how the trade currently felt (winning, "still looks
strong"), not because any new structural evidence (Module 4) actually
supported a wider target (Lesson 3).

**5. Answer: B) The urge to enter a trade specifically because a move is
already happening, driven by the discomfort of not being part of it.**
FOMO isn't a calculation error (ruling out A), isn't itself a memory-based
cognitive bias like the ones in Lesson 7 (ruling out C), and is a
distinct pattern from revenge trading, which is specifically about
recovering a loss rather than missing a move (ruling out D) (Lesson 4).

**6. Answer guide.** The Forex market runs continuously and new setups
form constantly — one missed trade is, at most, one missed opportunity
among many that will follow, not a permanently closed door on trading
altogether. Treating any single setup as "the" opportunity is what makes
FOMO feel urgent, and it's rarely actually true (Lesson 4).

**7. Answer: revenge trading.** Per Lesson 5, the second trade should
never have been treated as responsible for recovering the first loss —
each trade should be evaluated independently, on its own merits against
the trader's actual criteria; instead, the trader should have accepted
the first loss as closed and waited for the next setup that genuinely
met their criteria (Lesson 5).

**8. Answer: B) It would meet the trader's criteria even if they had
taken zero other trades that day.** How long it's been since the last
trade (A), how quiet the market feels (C), and how marginal nearby
setups look (D) are all exactly the kind of impatience-driven reasoning
Lesson 6 warns against — none of them make a setup more valid (Lesson 6).

**9. Answer guide.** Any one of the five remaining biases from Lesson 7,
explained correctly: recency bias (overweighting recent results;
countermeasure: evaluate over a larger sample), loss aversion (feeling
losses more intensely than equivalent gains; countermeasure: predefine
stop and target before entering), overconfidence (overestimating your own
judgment after recent wins; countermeasure: keep position sizing fixed
regardless of recent results), outcome bias (judging a decision by its
result rather than its process; countermeasure: evaluate every trade
against whether it followed the process), or availability bias
(overweighting vivid or recent events; countermeasure: rely on written
records rather than memory) (Lesson 7).

**10. Answer: confirmation bias.** The countermeasure is deliberately
stating, out loud or in writing, what evidence would prove the current
view wrong, and specifically checking for it — in this scenario, checking
whether the lower high the trader wasn't mentioning had actually formed
(Lesson 7).

**11. Answer: False.** Per Lesson 8, a planned loss (proper setup,
sizing, and stop) and a careless loss (a skipped or violated step) are
different events, even when the dollar amount lost happens to be
identical — the difference is in the process, not the outcome
(Lesson 8).

**12. Answer guide.** Discipline means consistently acting according to a
predefined process, specifically in moments where doing so is
uncomfortable. Example of a disciplined decision: passing on a setup
that only partially matches entry criteria. Example of an impulsive
decision in the same kind of situation: taking that partial setup anyway
because it's "close enough" (Lesson 9).

**13. Answer: False.** Per Lesson 10, consistency of process and
consistency of results are different things — a trader can follow an
identical, disciplined process across many trades and still get a mix of
wins and losses, exactly as Module 6's expectancy concept would predict
(Lesson 10).

**14. Answer guide.** Any four of: Am I following my actual setup
criteria, or making an exception? Am I entering because of a genuine
signal or an emotion? Am I trying to recover a previous loss? Am I afraid
of missing the move? Is my planned risk actually acceptable? Would I
still take this exact trade if I had taken no other trades today?
(Lesson 9).

**15. Answer guide.** Any three of: What did I plan? What did I actually
do? Did I follow my rules? What emotion was present? Did that emotion
change my decision? What should I repeat next time? What should I change?
In this specific case, the trader's review should surface that the stop
was moved out of discomfort rather than logic, which is the specific rule
violation to note (Lesson 12).

**16. Answer: False.** Per Lesson 13, a winning streak tends to produce
overconfidence, which is exactly when a rule violation is most likely to
slip through unnoticed — the same risk and rule discipline from Module 6
and Lesson 9 applies regardless of how recent trades have gone
(Lesson 13).

**17. Answer: Date, Situation, Emotion, Rule Violated, Consequence,
Corrective Action.** (Lesson 14).
