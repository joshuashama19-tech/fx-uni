# Module 7 — Exercises

These exercises apply what you covered in `01-content.md`. They use new
scenarios, not the exact examples from the lessons, so you're applying
the frameworks, not recalling a result. Work through them in order.
Answers aren't provided here on purpose (as with Modules 1–6): this is
practice, not another quiz. Several of these exercises ask you to reflect
honestly on your own tendencies — there's no way to get that kind of
question "wrong," but there's also no benefit to answering it
dishonestly.

## Exercise 1 — Scenario analysis: fear

A trader has a position open that's moving in their favor, approaching
their planned take-profit level from Module 4. As it gets close, they
start worrying the gain will disappear, and close the trade manually
several pips before the actual target.

1. Which specific fear-driven behavior from Lesson 2 does this match?
2. What was actually controllable in this situation, and what wasn't?
3. What would a disciplined version of this decision have looked like?

## Exercise 2 — Scenario analysis: greed

A trader's position hits their planned take-profit level exactly. Instead
of closing it, they decide to let it run further because "it might keep
going," with no new structural reason (Module 4) for the change.

1. Which specific greed-driven behavior from Lesson 3 does this match?
2. Was this decision based on process or on how the trade currently felt?
3. Per Lesson 3, is wanting to grow an account itself the problem here?
   Explain the distinction.

## Exercise 3 — Identifying emotional triggers

For each short scenario below, name the primary emotional pattern at work
(fear, greed, FOMO, revenge trading, or impatience/overtrading):

1. "I entered immediately after seeing three people post about a huge
   move, even though I had no plan for this pair today."
2. "I lost a trade an hour ago, and I just took a much larger position
   than usual on the very next setup I saw."
3. "Nothing has met my criteria in four hours, so I took a setup that
   barely qualifies just to have a position on."
4. "I skipped a setup that matched all my criteria because my last two
   trades lost and I didn't trust it."
5. "My trade was up nicely, so I moved my target further away because I
   didn't want to leave money on the table."

## Exercise 4 — Identifying cognitive biases

For each short scenario below, name the specific bias from Lesson 7
(confirmation bias, recency bias, loss aversion, overconfidence, outcome
bias, or availability bias) at work:

1. "My last four trades all won, so I'm sure my read on the market right
   now is especially sharp."
2. "I took a trade outside my rules and it won, so I've decided it was
   actually a good decision."
3. "I keep thinking about the one huge loss from two months ago far more
   than the twenty ordinary trades since then."
4. "I'm holding this losing position because closing it means admitting
   the loss is real."
5. "I only look at chart evidence that supports the trade I already
   want to take."
6. "My last three trades lost, so I've concluded my whole strategy
   doesn't work" (after only those three trades).

## Exercise 5 — Distinguishing planned vs. impulsive decisions

For each decision below, state whether it's a disciplined (planned)
decision or an impulsive one, and briefly explain why, using Lesson 9's
comparison table:

1. Passing on a setup that only partially matches entry criteria.
2. Moving a stop loss further away because price is approaching it.
3. Waiting for the next valid setup after a losing trade.
4. Entering a trade specifically because a previous loss needs to be
   recovered.
5. Exiting a trade at the exact price level planned before entry.

## Exercise 6 — The FOMO decision

A trader watches a currency pair rally 80 pips in under an hour, with no
valid entry trigger (Module 4) having formed before the move started.
They feel a strong urge to enter immediately at the current price.

1. Walk through Lesson 4's Emotional Pause process (Notice → Stop →
   Identify → Check Rules → Decide) as it would apply to this exact
   moment.
2. What should the trader do if, after "Check Rules," the current price
   genuinely does not meet their entry criteria?
3. Per Lesson 4, why is missing this specific move not the same as
   missing "the market" as a whole?

## Exercise 7 — Revenge trading escalation

A trader takes a properly planned, correctly sized trade that hits its
stop loss. Feeling frustrated, they immediately look for another setup,
find one that only loosely matches their criteria, and take it anyway
at 1.5× their normal position size specifically to recover the first
loss faster.

1. At which specific step did this situation first start following the
   pattern Lesson 5 describes?
2. Per Lesson 5, why shouldn't the second trade have been responsible for
   recovering the first loss?
3. What should the trader have done differently after the first loss
   closed?

## Exercise 8 — Rule-violation log practice

Using Lesson 14's Rule-Violation Log structure (Date, Situation, Emotion,
Rule Violated, Consequence, Corrective Action), fill in one complete,
realistic row based on Exercise 7's revenge-trading scenario above.

## Exercise 9 — Applying the Pre-Trade Psychology Check

A trader is about to enter a trade. The setup technically meets their
entry criteria, but they notice they're also feeling anxious about
having missed the last two profitable moves.

Answer each of Lesson 9's six Pre-Trade Psychology Check questions for
this specific situation, and conclude whether the trader should proceed
with the trade as planned or pause first.

## Exercise 10 — Post-trade review practice

A trader entered a trade that matched their criteria, but moved their
stop loss further away mid-trade out of discomfort as price approached
it. The trade eventually closed at a larger loss than originally planned.

Using Lesson 12's seven Post-Trade Review questions, write a complete
review of this trade.

## Exercise 11 — Personal trigger map

Reflect honestly on your own trading (or, if you're new to live trading,
your reactions in general to loss, missed opportunity, and success).
For each of the five patterns below, write one or two sentences on
whether you recognize this tendency in yourself, and in what kind of
situation it's most likely to show up:

1. Fear (hesitation, early exits, moved stops)
2. Greed (oversized positions, moved targets)
3. FOMO (chasing moves already in progress)
4. Revenge trading (sizing up after a loss)
5. Overtrading/impatience (forcing trades during quiet periods)

## Exercise 12 — Writing personal psychology rules

Using Lesson 14's examples as a model (not a list to copy), write four
personal psychology rules of your own, each addressing a specific pattern
you identified in Exercise 11. Each rule should be specific enough that
you could actually check, after the fact, whether you followed it.

## Exercise 13 — Designing a daily routine

Using Lesson 12's Before / During / After structure, write out a complete
personal trading routine. For each of the three phases, list at least
three specific actions you would actually take — not generic statements
like "stay calm," but concrete steps like Lesson 12's own examples
(checking the economic calendar, reviewing entry criteria, recording
trades).

## Exercise 14 — Spot the mistake

Each short scenario below contains one of the beginner mistakes covered
in this module. Identify the mistake and briefly explain what the trader
should have done differently.

1. "I lost a well-planned trade today, so I feel like a bad trader."
2. "I know exactly what confirmation bias is, so it doesn't really affect
   my decisions."
3. "I've won eight trades in a row, so I bumped up my position size — my
   read on the market is clearly working right now."
4. "Nothing happened for three hours, so obviously something's wrong
   with my strategy and I need to lower my standards."
5. "I wrote my psychology rules when I started this module. I haven't
   looked at them since."
